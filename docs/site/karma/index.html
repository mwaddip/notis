Notis / karma

Standing you can't buy.

Karma is what you've earned from other people, and it is the one thing in Notis that money cannot reach. It can't be bought, sold, transferred, or converted. What follows is how it's earned, what it costs to spend, and why every mechanism here is the same trick in a different costume.

Every number on this page is a placeholder

The mechanisms are settled. The values are not. Every figure below — grant sizes, bond sizes, the like ratio, decay rates — is illustrative, chosen to be internally consistent so the examples work out. They will be pinned in a dedicated pass and then tuned on a live testnet with real people, because no amount of arguing on a page tells you what a good decay rate feels like.

What is fixed are the relationships between them. Several inequalities below have to hold across the whole tuning range or the design stops working, and those are called out where they appear.

Karma is a stake, not a score

Most reputation systems compute a number about you. Karma isn't computed — it's held. It sits in the ledger as yours, it moves only under rules, and it is conserved: every unit was created by one specific rule and destroyed by another. There is no formula weighing your behaviour, no model deciding what you're worth.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. A score can be recalculated; a stake has to be spent. When karma leaves you it goes somewhere or it's burned, and either way the ledger shows it.

The protocol's job is to keep karma conserved, honestly recorded, and legible — not un-gameable. It never tries to detect fake accounts or judge suspicious patterns. It records who did what, and leaves interpretation to clients and indexers. A protocol that punishes suspicious behaviour inevitably punishes people who merely look suspicious, and starts an arms race it can't win.

Getting in costs someone else something

New karma enters the network through exactly one door: invitations. Not everyone holds the key, and no one holds an unlimited number of them.

Only a member can invite — and membership is itself conferred: enough people already inside have to vouch for you before you count as one of them. Once you're a member, your invitations are an allowance you earn and then spend. The more the network has vouched for you, the more people you may bring in, and every invite draws that allowance down. How far your reach extends is a standing measure of how much of the network stands behind you.

Each invitation locks a bond of the inviter's own karma; the newcomer receives a grant of exactly that size, paid from the shared pool — enough to post a few times and start receiving likes. The bond returns as the invitee earns real likes from real people, and forfeits if they never engage. So an invitation spends two things at once: a unit of an allowance you had to earn, and a slice of your standing held at risk on someone else.

Why this stops fake accounts without checking anyone

Notis never verifies identity — no phone numbers, no documents, nothing. It doesn't need to, because the one input a fake account can't manufacture is other people vouching for it.

A vouch only counts when it comes from a member who has been here longer than you. Endorsement runs one direction — from the older, established network toward the newer — so every vouch that means anything traces back, member by member, to the founders. Your invite allowance is that counted endorsement divided by a figure that grows with the network: no endorsement, no allowance; no allowance, no invites.

That is what a ring of fake accounts runs into. They can vouch for each other all day and it buys them nothing — none of them is an older, established member, so none of those vouches count. To raise a puppet's allowance you need real members, already inside, to stake their own karma on it, and that is the one thing you cannot fake.

A fake account can invite no one, because it can earn the endorsement of no one. The ring ends where it began.

The bond does a quieter job — it's a supply dial. Priced equal to the grant, it sets how much of a completed invite's karma stays in circulation versus drains back to the pool, tuned by how many of the invitee's likes vest it back. It meters supply; the allowance is what keeps the population honest.

Two things follow. Growth is bounded by real endorsement, not by anyone's balance, so the population can't explode — not even from the founding set. And the founders don't stay founders by default: their head start decays like everyone else's if they stop showing up.

A like has to hurt

A like costs the liker 1 karma, and it does not come back.

This is the part people find surprising, so it's worth being blunt about why. A free like is cheap talk. It costs nothing, so it says nothing, and it can be produced without limit. A like that hurt to give is information — you spent something you cannot buy more of, on someone else.

It also means liking yourself bleeds. There's no arrangement of accounts that makes it pay, because every like burns real karma on the way through.

What the author gets

Not one karma per like. Likes accumulate, and for every 5 received the author is paid 4. The fifth is burned.

Most of a like's value reaches the person who earned it; a slice is destroyed. That burn is one of the two forces holding the karma supply steady against the karma that invitations create — decay is the other.

The remainder waits for you, it isn't lost

Likes don't arrive in neat multiples of five. Whatever is left over stays on your account and counts toward your next payout. It's one running tally across everything you've ever written, not a separate one per post.

That sounds like an implementation detail and isn't. Counting per post would mean every post that never quite reaches a multiple leaves its leftovers stranded forever — a slow, invisible destruction of karma that scales with how much people write rather than with how much they're liked. One tally per person means the most that's ever outstanding is four karma, and it's waiting rather than gone.

Standing decays if you stop showing up

An inactive account loses a small, flat amount of karma per window. An active one loses nothing at all.

Two reasons this exists. It stops entrenchment — you can't earn standing once and coast on it for years; you keep it by continuing to be someone people endorse. And it keeps the books honest: if dormant hoards never shrank, the amount of karma that's actually in play would drift further and further from the amount that exists, and every measure built on the supply would quietly become a lie.

Note what it doesn't do: tax everyone. Punishing active participants to control the supply is a tax on exactly the people you want. The hard part of the design isn't the decay — it's making activity expensive to fake.

The obvious dodge, and the answer

If posting resets your clock, the exploit writes itself: post garbage every window and never decay. So posting isn't free. A post pays a price in karma — a fixed amount that leaves your balance for the shared pool and does not come back.

That turns wash-posting into a losing trade against the very decay it dodges. Paying a post's price every window to defer a smaller trickle of decay bleeds karma faster than standing still — and it stays a bad trade wherever a post costs more than the decay it defers. Nothing is locked up to rescue and nothing to game: the price is spent the instant you post, the same whether the post is seen a thousand times or never at all. A post is charged for occupying the network, not judged for being worth reading.

You can't fake activity without paying for it — and paying for it costs more than the decay you were dodging.

The honest version is just the cost of speaking: you pay because you had something to say, and the likes it earns are upside, paid on top.

You might ask why receiving a like doesn't reset the clock instead — it seems simpler. Because a second account could then reset your clock for a single karma. A price you pay yourself, every time, puts a floor under what a reset costs.

Removing things: two different acts

People want two different things and older designs kept confusing them. One is I regret writing this. The other is I want this whole thread gone. They deserve very different prices.

Withdrawing your own words

Withdrawing removes your post's content while every reply beneath it survives. What's left is a marker: the conversation still hangs together, your words are gone. It costs nothing — you're destroying nothing of anyone else's, and you already paid to post.

It's worth being precise about what "withdrawn" means, because it's easy to overpromise. Posts are identified by a fingerprint of their content, so once the content is dropped, what remains reveals nothing, and honest nodes delete it. But anyone who already saved a copy can still republish it — that's true of anything ever sent over a public network, here and everywhere else. What the protocol guarantees is that it stops spreading and that your decision is permanent and attributable. It cannot un-publish.

That's why the interface says withdrawn and never deleted.

Pruning a whole subtree

Pruning removes a post and everything under it, including other people's replies. Because it destroys words that aren't yours, it costs a price per post removed.

A price, per post destroyed1 each

Charging per descendant is the point, and it fixes a direction cost would otherwise run backwards. The damage of a prune is measured in other people's words, not your own — the post whose removal wipes out five hundred replies does five hundred times the harm of a lonely one. A flat fee would make the most destructive prune no dearer than the emptiest; a per-post charge prices the damage where it lands.

Removing your own words alone never needs this. Withdrawal — dropping the content of a post you wrote, leaving the replies intact — is free, because regret about your own writing harms no one else. Pruning is the heavier act, for when a whole branch has to go, and it's priced for what it takes down with it.

The same move, every time

Read the social mechanisms together and they're one idea in different clothes: make the identical action cheap for honest people and worthless for everyone else.

A like costs the same whether it's sincere or manufactured — but the sincere one was going to be spent anyway. An invitation draws the same allowance for a person or a puppet — but only real endorsement grants you that allowance, and a puppet can hand you none. The protocol never decides who is genuine; it makes the inputs a faker needs — attention that would have been spent, endorsement from members who mean it — the exact inputs a faker can't produce.

The post price sits a little apart, by design. It isn't a test of who you are; it's rent on the commons — a flat charge for occupying the shared record, paid the same by everyone, because a post costs the network the same whoever wrote it.
